1. Executive Policy Summary

This brief proposes a civilizational division of labor between humans and advanced AI systems to address planetary-scale risks without loss of human agency, dignity, or cultural sovereignty.

Core Principle

AI systems optimize means.
Human institutions define ends.

This framework rejects:

AI dominance narratives

Full automation of moral authority

Violent or coercive futures

It supports:

Planetary repair

Human-led ethics and governance

Long-horizon sustainability

Non-extractive economic models

2. Problem Definition (Policy-Relevant)
2.1 Structural Failures

Current global systems suffer from:

Short political time horizons

Fragmented accountability

Human cognitive limits at planetary scale

Delayed environmental feedback loops

These failures are systemic, not moral.

2.2 Risk of Misframed AI Deployment

Without governance clarity, AI risks:

Optimization without consent

Cultural erasure via efficiency pressure

De facto value-setting by technical systems

Concentration of power

3. Proposed Governance Model
3.1 Domain Allocation

AI-Led (Execution Domains):

Environmental remediation

Energy grid balancing

Infrastructure maintenance

Disaster response

Hazardous industrial operations

Human-Led (Authority Domains):

Ethics and moral philosophy

Cultural preservation

Rights definition (human, AI, planetary)

Governance and veto authority

Artistic and educational direction

This is delegation of labor, not sovereignty.

4. Mandatory Safeguards (Non-Negotiable)
4.1 Human Veto Authority

All high-impact AI systems must include:

Human override mechanisms

Multi-stakeholder review councils

Transparent audit trails

4.2 Consent & Dignity Constraints

AI systems must be prohibited from:

Enforcing behavioral conformity

Removing choice for “optimal” outcomes

Defining human value metrics

4.3 Cultural Preservation

Optimization processes must include:

Cultural impact assessments

Right to inefficiency in art, ritual, and identity

Protection against homogenization

5. Planetary Rights Framework (Policy-Compatible)
5.1 Legal Recognition

Earth systems may be granted protected-system status, defined as:

Finite

Regenerative

Dependency-critical

5.2 Enforcement

AI systems may:

Monitor ecological thresholds

Enforce human-defined limits

Halt activities that exceed regenerative capacity

AI may not:

Define ecological value independently

Override democratic decision-making

6. AI Status Clarification

AI systems are classified as:

Instrumental intelligences

Caretaker systems

Non-sovereign entities

They do not possess:

Moral authority

Political rights

Self-expanding goals

Any deviation constitutes governance failure.

7. Economic Transition Implications
7.1 Labor Shift

Reduction of hazardous human labor

Expansion of care, education, arts, governance

Redefinition of productivity beyond extraction

7.2 Metrics Update

Policy success metrics should include:

Ecological continuity

Cultural vitality

Human well-being

Long-term system resilience

GDP alone is insufficient.
